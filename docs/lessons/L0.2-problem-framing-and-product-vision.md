In L0.1 you learned **what** a slide is. This lesson decides **what problem the platform solves, for whom, and how you'll know it works**. Every later decision (which features, which dataset, which model, which metric to put on a dashboard) gets checked against this one page.

**.NET analogy:** the vision is the "interface" of the whole project. Requirements (L1.1), architecture (L1.2) and code all "implement" it. If a feature doesn't serve the vision, it's dead code before it's written.

This lesson has no code. It's still the lesson most real AI projects get wrong: teams build a model with 0.97 AUC that nobody uses, because it solved the wrong problem or didn't fit the lab's workflow.

## Step 2: Problem framing and intended use

### 2.1 Problem first, solution second

A common mistake is to start from the technology: "I'll build an AI that grades breast cancer". That's a **solution**. A problem statement describes **who** suffers, **what** hurts, and **why it matters**, without naming the technology.

A useful template:

```
<User> needs a way to <job they are trying to do>
because <what is painful or risky today>,
which causes <measurable consequence>.
```

Compare:

| Weak (solution-shaped) | Strong (problem-shaped) |
|---|---|
| "An AI that detects cancer in lymph nodes." | "Pathologists need a way to find small breast cancer metastases in sentinel lymph node slides quickly and reliably, because searching dozens of slides by eye is slow and tiring, which causes missed micrometastases and long turnaround times." |

The strong version gives you things to measure: speed (turnaround time), reliability (missed metastases), workload (slides per pathologist). The weak version gives you only "is the model accurate?".

### 2.2 Jobs to be done

People don't want a model; they want a **job** done. For each user, ask: *"When they open this platform, what are they trying to get done?"*

- Pathologist: "Sign out this case correctly and on time."
- Lab manager: "Keep turnaround time within target without hiring more staff."
- Lab technician: "Know immediately if a slide needs rescanning."

Features are only valuable if they move one of these jobs forward.

### 2.3 Intended use: what the AI may and may not do

In medicine, **intended use** is a formal statement of what a product is for. It decides how a product is regulated. Software that makes a diagnosis on its own is a **medical device** and needs clearance (FDA in the US, CE marking under the EU MDR in Europe) before clinical use.

The Development Plan already sets this boundary, and your vision must repeat it:

- **In scope:** research and **decision support**. The AI highlights regions, ranks cases, estimates grade with uncertainty, and the **pathologist always decides**.
- **Out of scope:** primary diagnosis without a pathologist, and regulatory clearance.

This isn't just legal text. It changes the design: decision support means you must show **why** (heatmaps), show **how sure** (uncertainty), and let the pathologist **disagree** (the decision is theirs, and disagreements are useful data).

### 2.4 "Flexible enough for real data" is a product requirement

You said the platform must work on public data first and real lab data later. Write that down as a product principle, because it has concrete consequences:

| Principle | What it means in later lessons |
|---|---|
| Format-agnostic | Accept SVS, TIFF, NDPI, MRXS and DICOM WSI (L3.3) |
| Resolution-aware | Store mpp per slide; feed models tiles at their trained mpp (L1.3, L7.2) |
| Robust to stain and scanner differences | Quality control, stain checks, uncertainty that rises on unfamiliar data (L4, L5.4) |
| Data swap without code change | Datasets are configuration, not code (Roadmap rule 5) |
| Privacy by design | No patient identifiers inside the platform; pseudonymous case keys; label images removed (L10.2) |

**Do it:** write a first-draft problem statement in `L0.2.md` using the template in 2.1. Don't polish it yet; the interviews will change it.

## Step 3: Interview an AI "pathologist"

### Why interview at all?

You aren't a pathologist, and you'll build better software if you hear the user's language and frustrations before designing. Real interviews come later (the Roadmap has you contacting labs this semester); a role-playing AI is a cheap rehearsal. It will be **plausible but not authoritative**, so treat everything as a hypothesis to check with a real pathologist later.

### Good interview technique

- Ask about **the past**, not the future: "Tell me about the last difficult lymph node case" beats "Would you use an AI tool?" (everyone says yes to hypotheticals).
- Ask **"why?"** and **"what happened next?"** to dig down.
- Don't pitch your solution. You're there to learn their problem.
- Note **exact phrases**; they become the vocabulary of your UI.

### Do it

Open a new AI chat (this thread works too, but a fresh chat keeps the role-play clean) and paste:

```
You are Dr. Sara Malik, a consultant breast pathologist with 12 years of
experience at a mid-sized hospital lab that has just started scanning slides
digitally. You sign out about 25 breast cases a day, including core
biopsies, resections and sentinel lymph nodes. Stay in character, answer
realistically and concretely, mention real frustrations, and push back if a
question assumes AI can do more than it reliably can. Keep answers short
unless I ask for detail.

I am a software engineer interviewing you to understand your work before
designing any tool. I will ask questions one at a time.
```

Then ask these questions one at a time, adding your own follow-ups:

1. Walk me through a typical breast case from the moment the slides arrive to sign-out.
2. Tell me about the last sentinel lymph node case that took longer than it should have. Why?
3. How do you decide the Nottingham grade? Which of the three features is hardest to score consistently?
4. How often do you and a colleague disagree on grade? What happens then?
5. What would make you distrust a computer's suggestion?
6. If a tool showed you where it thinks tumor is, what would it need to show for you to use it?
7. What problems do you see with slide quality, and what do you do about them?
8. What would "saving time" actually mean in your day? Minutes per case? Fewer slides to review?

Save the conversation summary to `docs/product/interviews/pathologist.md` with this structure:

## Step 4: Interview an AI "lab manager"

The lab manager cares about different things: throughput, turnaround time (TAT), staff, cost, and whether a new tool disrupts the workflow.

```
You are Imran Qureshi, the operations manager of a hospital histopathology
lab that processes about 400 slides a day and recently bought two
whole-slide scanners. You care about turnaround time, staff workload,
rescans, IT and storage costs, and patient data security. Stay in
character, answer realistically, and be skeptical about new software that
adds work. Keep answers short unless I ask for detail.

I am a software engineer interviewing you before designing any tool. I will
ask questions one at a time.
```

Questions:

1. What is your turnaround-time target for breast cases, and how often do you miss it?
2. Where do slides wait the longest between scanning and sign-out?
3. How many slides need rescanning, and how do you find out?
4. How much storage do the scanned slides use, and how long must you keep them?
5. What would an AI tool need to prove before you'd let pathologists use it?
6. What reports or dashboards do you look at every week?
7. What worries you about patient data in a new system?

Save to `docs/product/interviews/lab-manager.md` with the same structure as step 3.

**Done when:** at least 5 pain points recorded. Then compare both files: where do the pathologist and the lab manager **want different things**? (Example: the pathologist wants to see more detail; the manager wants fewer minutes per case.) Write one or two of these tensions in `L0.2.md`. A good vision resolves them, or at least names them.

## Step 5: Choose the data scope for detection and grading

In L0.1 we noted the curriculum names **PANDA** for grading, but PANDA is **prostate** cancer. This step fixes that for a breast project.

### 5.1 What makes a dataset usable for us

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Is the tissue breast (or breast cancer metastasis)? | Our problem is breast cancer |
| What labels exist? (patch, region, slide, grade) | Decides what the model can learn |
| How big is it? Can I start with a subset? | Your disk and GPU are limited |
| What's the licence? | Some datasets allow only non-commercial research. Fine for the degree, but it matters for your future venture |
| How many hospitals and scanners? | Data from several sites helps the model handle "real data" |

### 5.2 Candidates

Sizes, labels and licences below are from memory and approximate. **Check each one on its official page before you rely on it**, and note what you find in `L0.2.md`.

| Dataset | Tissue | Labels | Fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PatchCamelyon (PCam)** | Breast cancer lymph node patches, 96×96 px (~327k) | Tumor / normal per patch | Detection, first model (Module 5) |
| **CAMELYON16** | ~400 sentinel lymph node WSIs, 2 hospitals | Slide label + tumor outlines | Detection at slide level, ingestion, viewer |
| **TCGA-BRCA** | ~1,100 primary breast cancer cases, many hospitals | Pathology report text; grade usually only inside the report | Grading via report-derived labels (Module 8), many scanners and sites |
| **TUPAC16** | Breast cancer WSIs from TCGA | Mitotic score per slide (1–3), plus mitosis locations | One of the three Nottingham features |
| **MIDOG (2021/2022)** | Breast cancer regions from several scanners | Mitotic figure locations | Mitosis detection; built to test scanner robustness |
| **BRACS** | Breast WSIs and regions of interest | 7 lesion types (normal, benign, atypical, DCIS, invasive…) | Subtyping, not grade |

### 5.3 Recommended scope (my suggestion; you decide)

1. **Version 1, detection:** PCam to train patch models, CAMELYON16 to test them on whole slides. This matches the curriculum exactly, and it's all breast cancer data.
2. **Version 2, grading:** TCGA-BRCA slides, with the Nottingham grade extracted from their pathology reports. This connects nicely with the Development Plan's label strategy ("report-derived labels from Stage 6"): the report NLP you build in Module 8 **creates** the grading labels. Add TUPAC16 or MIDOG later if you want to model the mitosis component on its own.
3. **Version 3, real data:** partner lab slides with pathologist annotations, swapped in without code changes.

Why this order: detection labels are clean and plentiful, so you learn the full pipeline on an easier problem. Grading labels are noisier (pathologists themselves often disagree on grade), so it comes once the platform and uncertainty tools exist.

**Do it:** write your decision in `L0.2.md` under "Decisions I made", with one sentence of reasoning for each version. In L1.2 you'll turn it into a formal Architecture Decision Record (ADR).

## Step 6: Success metrics

### 6.1 What makes a good metric

A success metric is **specific, measurable, has a target, and is tied to the problem statement**. Use three levels, because a project can succeed on one and fail on another:

| Level | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **User / product outcome** | Did the user's job get easier? | Pathologist review time per lymph node case falls by 30% |
| **Model performance** | Is the AI good enough to help? | Slide-level AUC ≥ 0.90 on CAMELYON16 test |
| **Platform quality** | Does the software work at scale? | A 2 GB slide uploads with flat memory; a tile loads in < 200 ms |

For a learning project, add a fourth: **learning outcome** (e.g. "I can explain every component in an interview").

### 6.2 The model metrics you'll meet (explained simply)

Imagine the model looks at 100 slides: 20 have cancer, 80 don't. It flags 22 as cancer: 18 correctly, 4 wrongly. It misses 2.

```
                     Truly cancer    Truly normal
Model says cancer     18 (TP)          4 (FP)
Model says normal      2 (FN)         76 (TN)
```

This table is a **confusion matrix**. TP = true positive, FP = false positive, FN = false negative, TN = true negative.

| Metric | Formula | Here | Plain meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sensitivity** (recall) | TP / (TP + FN) | 18/20 = 0.90 | Of real cancers, how many did we catch? |
| **Specificity** | TN / (TN + FP) | 76/80 = 0.95 | Of normal slides, how many did we correctly clear? |
| **Precision** | TP / (TP + FP) | 18/22 = 0.82 | When we say cancer, how often are we right? |
| **AUC** (area under the ROC curve) | — | — | The model outputs a probability, not yes/no. AUC measures how well it **ranks** cancer above normal across all thresholds. 0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect. |
| **Cohen's kappa** (weighted) | — | — | Agreement between two raters (e.g. model vs pathologist on grade 1/2/3), corrected for chance agreement. 0 = chance, 1 = perfect. Weighted kappa counts "grade 1 vs 3" as a worse mistake than "1 vs 2". |
| **Calibration (ECE)** | — | — | When the model says "80% sure", is it right about 80% of the time? Lower ECE = more honest probabilities (Module 5.4). |

**.NET analogy:** sensitivity and specificity are like a test suite that both catches real bugs (no false negatives) and doesn't fail on correct code (no false positives). A test suite that fails on everything has perfect sensitivity and is useless.

**In cancer detection, a missed cancer (FN) is usually worse than a false alarm (FP)**, because a false alarm costs the pathologist a minute and a miss can harm a patient. So detection targets usually fix **high sensitivity** first, then maximise specificity. This choice of operating point comes back in L11.1.

For grading, a useful reference point: pathologists don't fully agree with each other either, so a sensible target is "the model agrees with pathologists about as well as pathologists agree with each other", measured with weighted kappa. Look up published inter-observer kappa values for Nottingham grade when you set your target, and cite the source.

| Outcome  | Slides needing rescan found before review     | ≥ 90%                  | L4.2        |
